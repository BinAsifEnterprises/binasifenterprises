5 reels every local business should post

Short video is often the first way a new customer meets your business. The good news: you do not need a studio, an expensive camera or acting skills. A phone, decent light and your real workplace are enough. Here are five simple reel ideas any local business can film.

1. The welcome reel

Stand at your door, counter or workshop bench and say hello. Tell people who you are, what you sell or do, and where to find you. It sounds basic, but most customers have never seen the person behind the business — and people feel more comfortable buying from a face they recognise.

2. The behind-the-scenes reel

Show the work before it reaches the customer: ingredients being prepared, a shelf being restocked, a style in progress, a repair on the bench. Everyday process footage feels honest because it is honest, and it quietly answers the question every customer has — do these people know what they are doing?

3. The close-up reel

Pick one product, dish or finished job and film it up close, slowly. Good light and a steady hand matter more than editing tricks. Close-ups let people judge the quality for themselves, which is exactly what they are trying to do before they visit, book or order.

4. The quick tip reel

Share one small piece of advice from your trade: how to keep flowers fresh for longer, how to choose the right size, what to check before booking a service. Tips mark you out as the expert in your area, and they give people a reason to follow you even when they are not buying today.

5. The question reel

Take a question customers ask you all the time and answer it on camera, in plain words. You already give that answer several times a week. Recording it once means it can keep answering for you — and it is often the video a new customer watches right before getting in touch.

Keep it simple, keep it going

You do not need all five in a single week. Film one, post it, and work through the set over time with new products, new work and new questions. And if filming and editing take time you simply do not have, that is exactly the kind of work we handle for local businesses.

Why consistent posting beats chasing viral

Every business owner has seen it: one post takes off, the phone lights up for a day, and then everything goes quiet again. Chasing that moment feels exciting, but it is a poor plan. A steady posting rhythm does the quieter, more valuable work.

Viral is a lottery, not a strategy

A viral post depends on timing, luck and an algorithm you do not control. You cannot schedule it, repeat it on demand or build a month of business around it. A sudden spike also tends to reach people far outside your area — people who will never walk through your door or place an order.

Consistency is how trust is built

Customers rarely buy the first time they see a business. They buy after seeing it a few times and getting a feel for it: the quality of the work, the tone, the people behind it. Regular posts create that familiarity. Each one is a small reminder that you are open, active and doing good work.

A rhythm also makes you better

Posting regularly gives you practice and honest feedback. You learn which products people ask about, which videos they watch to the end and which offers earn replies. That learning builds up over time. A business that posts every week keeps getting sharper; a business waiting for one perfect post learns nothing in between.

What a workable rhythm looks like

It does not need to be daily. A few posts a week, at roughly regular times, covering a simple mix — your work, your products, your people, your answers to common questions — is enough to stay present in people's minds. The best schedule is the one you can still be keeping three months from now.

The bottom line

If keeping that rhythm is the hard part, it usually is not a creativity problem — it is a time problem. Planning, filming and publishing on schedule, week after week, is the core of social media management. It is unglamorous work, and it is the work that builds a presence customers can rely on.

Human content vs AI content: when to use each

AI tools can now generate images, video and voice in minutes, and plenty of businesses are asking the same question: should we still film real content? The honest answer is that the two do different jobs — and used in the right places, they make each other stronger.

Where real, human content wins

Anything that depends on trust belongs on camera. Your premises, your team, your hands doing the work, a customer question answered in your own voice — these things prove you are real, local and accountable. For services people invite into their homes, or trust with their appearance, health or money, real footage carries a weight that generated imagery simply cannot.

Where AI content genuinely helps

AI is useful where a camera cannot easily go: visualising a product that does not exist yet, creating a concept visual for an idea you are testing, building an eye-catching backdrop for a promotion, or producing a clean voiceover when recording conditions are poor. It is also a fast, low-cost way to try an idea before spending money on a full shoot.

A simple way to mix them

Here is a practical rule: let real content carry your identity, and let AI content carry your imagination. Film the people, the place and the finished work. Use AI for concepts, mock-ups, supporting visuals and extra versions of ideas that already work in real footage. Keep your branding — colours, logo, tone — consistent across both, so everything still looks and sounds like one business.

One honest caution

Audiences are getting better at spotting content that feels artificial. If a post could mislead someone about what your product, food or premises actually look like, do not post it. Trust is the whole point of being on social media at all — content that wins a click but costs trust turns out expensive in the long run.

How we approach it

Our own approach is human-led: real footage and real creative judgment first, AI tools where they genuinely add something a camera cannot. Neither extreme — all AI or no AI — serves a small business as well as a deliberate mix chosen post by post.

How often should your business post on social media?

It is usually the first question a business owner asks us: how often should we post? The honest answer is that there is no magic number. The best posting frequency is the one your business can keep up every week without burning out — because long gaps hurt more than a modest schedule ever will.

Consistency beats volume

Three good posts a week, every week, build more trust than ten posts in one busy week followed by a month of silence. Customers notice rhythm. A page that shows up regularly looks open, active and worth dealing with; a page that keeps disappearing looks closed, even when it is not. When you are unsure, choose the smaller schedule you can actually keep.

A simple weekly rhythm

Here is a pattern most busy small businesses can hold. One useful tip post — a small piece of advice from your trade that helps a customer. One behind-the-scenes or product post, showing the real work or the thing itself. And one reel, filmed on your phone, because short video is often the first way new people meet your business. On the days in between, add stories when — and only when — there is something real to show.

Batch it

Creating something new every single day is exhausting, and it is the fastest route to giving up altogether. Instead, sit down once a week and make the whole week's content in one sitting: write the captions, choose the photos, film the reel. One focused session replaces the daily scramble, and the work usually comes out better for it.

Reuse across platforms

One good idea does not have to live in just one place. A tip can become a reel, then a still post, then a story later in the week. That is the same work, seen in more places, by more of the people who might buy from you. You are not repeating yourself — you are making your effort count.

Watch the right signals

Likes are easy to collect and easy to overrate. Pay closer attention to replies, saves, shares and enquiries — the signals that show a post actually moved someone to act. Notice which of your posts earn those, and quietly do more of that kind. Over time, let your own customers, not guesswork, shape the rhythm.

Keep it simple, keep it going

None of this needs to be perfect. A simple rhythm, kept week after week, will carry a small business further than any burst of enthusiasm followed by silence.

INSIGHTS · PLANNING2 min read

The three-post week: a rhythm busy owners can keep

It is one of the first questions business owners ask us, and the honest answer is simpler than most advice suggests: post as often as you can do well, consistently. There is no magic number that suits every business — but there is a rhythm that will suit yours.

Start with three good posts a week

A few quality posts a week will always beat daily posts rushed out just to fill the feed. If you are starting from scratch, try this simple mix of three. One post showing your work — a finished job, a product, something a customer can judge for themselves. One helpful tip from your trade, the kind of small advice you already give people face to face. And one offer or behind-the-scenes post, so people see both what you sell and how you work. Three posts, three different reasons to remember you.

Every post is a small promise

Quality and consistency beat volume because every post quietly makes a promise: we are here, we are active, and this is the standard of our work. A weak post weakens that promise, and the pattern customers notice most is the burst followed by silence — a busy week of posting, then nothing for a month. That stop-and-start rhythm undoes trust, because a quiet page can look like a closed business, even when it is not.

Make the rhythm easy to keep

A schedule only works if a busy owner can actually keep it. Film in batches: set aside one day, or even one hour, and record several short videos in a single session instead of hunting for time every day. Keep a simple running list of ideas on your phone — questions customers ask, jobs you finish, small tips — so you never sit down to a blank page. And when a topic does well, reuse it in a new format: a tip that worked as a written post can come back as a reel, a photo or a story. Good ideas deserve more than one outing.

When it makes sense to post more

There are moments when posting more often is exactly right: a launch, a busy season for your trade, a limited offer, an event coming up. Raising the pace for a short, clear reason works, because the extra posts have something real to say. Treat those busy stretches as a campaign with an end date, then return to your normal rhythm — rather than trying to hold the higher pace forever and burning out.

If keeping up is the hard part

For most businesses the hard part is not ideas. It is the planning, filming, writing and posting, week after week, alongside running the business itself. That planning-and-posting work is exactly what social media management handles: a rhythm set in advance, content made in batches, and posts going out on schedule whether your week is quiet or chaotic.

What to post when you have nothing to post

Every business owner knows the feeling. You open your phone to post, and nothing comes to mind. No new product, no big news, nothing that feels worth sharing. So you post nothing — and a quiet week turns into a quiet month. The fix is not a better idea. It is looking at the work you have already done.

1. Show the work, not the advert

A finished job, a detail close-up, a before-and-after. Customers trust what they can see far more than what they are told. One honest photo of real work beats a polished graphic about it.

2. Answer a question a customer asked you this week

If one customer asked it, others are wondering the same thing. Turn the answer into a short post. It is useful, it is real, and it shows you listen.

3. Share a small lesson from a job

What went well, what took longer than expected, what you would do differently next time. People follow businesses that are honest about the work, not just the finished result.

4. Introduce the people and the process

Who does the work, how a job starts, what happens behind the scenes. Customers buy from people they feel they know, and a short look behind the curtain builds that familiarity.

5. Reuse your best post with a fresh angle

If a post helped someone once, it will help someone again. New followers have never seen it, and old followers rarely remember it. Update the example, change the opening line, and post it again.

Start with one this week

You do not need a new idea every day. You need a few honest ones, used well. Start with one of these five this week — the next idea usually arrives while you are making the first one.

How to brief a content agency in 15 minutes

Hiring someone to make your content should not start with a long meeting or a blank document. A clear brief fits inside a single message — and it is the difference between content that sounds like your business and content that could belong to anyone. Here is the 15-minute version, built on the four things we ask every client for.

1. Your goal — one sentence

Start with what you want the content to do: fill tables on quiet weekdays, sell a new product, bring in more enquiries, look more established. One clear goal beats a list of five, because every post can then be checked against it — does this move us toward the goal, or is it just noise? If you are not sure which goal matters most, say so; choosing it is part of the first conversation.

2. Your audience — who it is for

Tell us who you want to reach: where they live, roughly how old they are, and what they care about when they choose a business like yours. You know your customers better than anyone ever will. A post written for young parents reads differently from one written for office teams — and it should. Two honest lines about your audience are worth more than a page of guesswork.

3. Examples you like

Send two or three links or screenshots of posts, reels or pages you admire — yours or someone else’s. You do not need design vocabulary to describe a style. Examples show us your taste faster than any description can, and they stop everyone designing in a direction you would never have chosen. Dislikes help too: one example of what you never want is a useful boundary.

4. Your deadline

Say when you need the first batch: a launch date, an opening, a seasonal offer, an event. A real deadline lets us plan backwards and tell you honestly what fits in the time. It also sets up the rhythm after that first batch — the monthly calendar your content then follows, approved by you in advance.

Not sure yet? Start with the free audit

If you cannot fill in all four points, send what you have. Our free audit looks at your profile, your branding and your recent posts, and comes back with clear next steps — and a 15-minute brief often writes itself from there. A short brief sent today is worth more than a perfect brief next month.

Reel covers that stop the scroll

Before anyone watches your reel, they see its cover — in your grid, in search results, in a feed full of other businesses asking for the same second of attention. The cover is the headline of the video. Here is how we design one that earns the tap.

Write the headline first

A cover works when it promises one thing, in a few words a stranger understands instantly: the dish, the result, the question the reel answers. If the words only make sense after watching, they are a caption, not a headline. In our own process — the same one in our “Watch an Idea Become Content” storyboard — the cover is planned at the storyboard stage, before filming, so the shot and the words are designed together rather than patched up afterwards.

Design for the smallest size

Most people first meet your cover as a small thumbnail in a grid, surrounded by other businesses asking for the same second of attention. Test yours at that size: can you still read every word without zooming in? If not, the fix is fewer words and larger type — never a cleverer font. One short line, set large, beats three lines set small, every time.

One subject, clearly framed

Pick the single most recognisable thing in the reel — the dish, the product, the finished result — and let it fill the frame. A busy cover with five elements competing reads as noise at thumbnail size. A clean background and one strong subject give the eye somewhere to land, and give the words room to be read.

Keep your covers recognisably yours

Use the same colours, type and layout family across your covers, and people start recognising your posts before they have read a word. That consistency is quiet, but it compounds: your grid begins to look like one business with a point of view, instead of a pile of unrelated videos that happen to share an account.

Check it where it will live

Before posting, look at the cover in your profile grid, next to your last few posts. Does it sit comfortably with them? Is anything important hidden behind the buttons the platform draws over it? A ten-second check at the end protects the cover you spent real effort designing.

What to do with a post that flopped

Every business has posted something that landed with a thud — a couple of likes, no comments, silence. It feels worse than it is. A quiet post is not a verdict on your business; it is information. Here is the calm way to use it.

First, check the hook

Most posts are decided in the first line or the first second. Did yours open with the interesting part, or with background a reader had to wade through before reaching the point? If the hook buried the point, the post did not fail — the opening did. That is the easiest thing in the world to fix next time.

Then, check the cover

Look at the post where your audience actually saw it: small, in a busy feed, surrounded by louder things. Could the cover be read at a glance? Did it promise one clear thing? A good video with an unreadable cover is a book with the title missing — the content never really got its chance.

Check the posting time

Was your audience even around when it went out? A post aimed at parents that lands during the school run, or one aimed at office workers that lands in the middle of a meeting, starts life uphill. Compare the time with when your followers usually respond to you, and adjust the next post — small shifts, tested one at a time, so you know what actually changed.

Read the caption like a stranger

Does the caption ask for anything — a reply, a save, a visit, a message — or does it only describe the photo? Posts that end with a genuine question or one clear next step give people something to do. Silence often means nothing more than this: the post never asked.

Rework it before you repeat it

A flopped post is raw material, not rubbish. Take the same idea, give it a stronger hook and a clearer cover, and post the improved version a few weeks later — hardly anyone saw the first attempt, and the idea itself may have been right all along. And if the same format flops twice, retire it without regret and spend that energy on the formats your audience does answer.

Planning December content: a calm checklist for busy owners

December arrives quickly, and for most businesses it is the busiest trading month and the hardest month to keep posting in. The answer is not more content — it is a short plan, made once, while things are still calm. Here is the checklist we would work through.

1. Decide your December offers

Settle your seasonal offers early and write them down: what is included, when they start, and when they end. Content made from a written offer comes out simple and clear; content invented on the day usually does not. One offer, explained well and repeated calmly, beats five rushed ones your customers cannot keep track of.

2. Post your opening hours before anyone asks

Put your holiday opening hours into a post early in the month, and pin it or save it to your highlights so it stays findable all December. Add the dates that matter to your customers: last dates for orders and bookings, and any days you are closed. Every customer who finds the answer on your page is one less unanswered message in your busiest week.

3. Make a simple gift guide

If you sell anything that can be given, make it easy to give: a short guide that matches what you offer to the people your customers buy for — parents, colleagues, the friend who has everything. You are not inventing new work; you are answering the question every December customer is already asking, in the place they are already looking.

4. Say thank you, once, properly

Set aside one post to thank the people who kept you busy this year: customers, regulars, the team behind the counter. No offer attached, nothing to sell. It is the easiest post on this list to make, and often the warmest one you will publish all year.

5. Batch it early, then let the plan carry you

Film and design December’s posts in one or two sessions at the start of the month, schedule them, and let the plan carry you through the busy weeks. A calm feed in December is not luck — it is a short checklist, finished while things were still quiet.
